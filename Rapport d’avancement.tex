\section{Thesis topic}
\subsection{Thesis title}
Exploitation of historical analog seismological records by image processing and machine learning

\subsection{Research focus}
My PhD thesis is focused on processing of paper-based scanned seismograms using machine learning (ML) tools. Seismograms are the key data source in computational geophysics and seismology. Historical seismograms recorded in \nth{20} century form valuable data pool to evaluate past ground motions and seismicity of the Earth. However, such data mostly remain as old papers or in a raster format (scanned images), which should be vectorised to enable reuse. At the same time, manual vectorisation is a highly time‐consuming and cumbersome work, prone to human-induced errors, which often leads to misclassification of seismic traces. To address these limitations, we developed the Python-based algorithm of automatic vectorisation of the seismograms. 

\subsection{Research questions}
The research questions that we address in my PhD thesis include the following ones:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item What are the efficient ML methods for automatic vectorising of seismograms? 
    \item Which libraries and embedded algorithms of Python language can be applied for automatic vectorisation ?
    \item How to assess and interpret signals using seismic data in a semi-automated regime? 
    \item How to effectively process and organise massive datasets containing large amounts of seismograms ?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research problem} 

The research problem consists in developing effective method for vectorisation of seismograms. Seismograms are the key source of information in many geophysical applications and Earth science domains, such as earthquake modelling \cite{Kameda}, risk assessment \cite{Frankel,Panakkat}, civil engineering \cite{Savvaidis}, soil studies \cite{Papadakis}, geological simulations on rock and mineral physics, porosity and rheology \cite{Singhetal2020,DiMartino}. The analysis of the seismograms can be used for reconstructions of the past seismicity of the Earth, retrospective data modelling and earthquake hazard prediction \cite{Zhuetal2021}.  

So far, there is no effective method for robust vectorisation of seismograps. There are existing software for digitising seismograms, their solutions. For instance, Bogiatzis and Ishii developed the \emph{DigitSeis} \cite{Bogiatzis} aimed to classify features on seismograms, discriminating noise and generating time series to assign timing. Pintore et al. \cite{Pintoreetal2005} developed the \emph{Teseo} package where curves are recognised using colour contrast, approximated and vectorised. However, these software have technical limitations and drawbacks. For example, the success in vectorising seismograms by these software requires high quality images as input data. Ideally, they should have a distinct colour on the paper, contrasting and clearly recognised lines, no defects and faults, blurs and spots, distinguishable line width and continuity, identical time gaps or marks and line styles (curvature, repetitive chains of segments). In case of a perfect image, using such tools may lead to the acceptable outputs. Nevertheless, in reality old scanned images are almost often imperfect and need using machine learning algorithm for an automatic vectorisation that could ignore the imperfectness of the old image. 

In contrast, a ML-based algorithm can vectorise blurred or imperfect images, that is, those that contain spots and stains, where manual vectorising fails. Besides the questions of paper quality, processing the seismograms involves analysis of their amplitude, record time and signals source, originating from horizontal or vertical components. Therefore, it becomes a cumbersome task to vectorise images using traditional software. Automated workflow outperforms such methods by recognising features contained in a specific format of seismograms using computer vision and ML algorithms. This is possible using raster-to-vector (R2V) digitising methods.

Many R2V methods were proposed for optimisation of the vectorisation algorithms. Some of them employ ML for improving the accuracy and accelerating the speed of vectorising \cite{SilvaCoira,Mineter,Tafoya,Doucette}. Along with the progress in programming, such methods gradually improved and resulted in a variety of the developed algorithms of vectorisation, e.g., threshold morphology \cite{Janssen1997}, real-time line extraction \cite{Pferd,FULFORD198165}, topological analysis \cite{GROSS1995370}, approximation \cite{StanberryBesag,WallDanielsson}, 3D reconstruction \cite{Ceylan}. More examples of R2V algorithms include recognising the lines and discriminating them from the white/light background by line thinning, finding edges and neighbouring nodes \cite{Suzuki}, contrasting line width and smoothing shapes \cite{BaoFu}, iterative line optimisation and segmentation by CNN \cite{Inoue}, layering image and segmenting skeletons \cite{Hilaire}, extracting contours by simplification \cite{San,Chenetal2006}.

Most often recording earthquake strength \cite{Krishna2006}, they can also indicate other types of disasters, e.g., avalanches, tsunami, volcanic eruptions \cite{Vijaya2015}. Furthermore, signals strength and amplitudes of seismic waves recorded in seismograms are used to determine the ground motion of the Earth using specific properties and nature of seismic waves which includes the geometry of movements \cite{Lamb,Liuetal2016,Mueller,Fryer}. 
 
Records of the horizontal components in seismograms show regional heterogeneity and variations in the elastic parameters of the Earth's mantle \cite{Melchior}. \subsection{Objective}
During the \nth{1} thesis year (2021/2022) we addressed a problem of vectorising seismograms by a novel algorithm for automated vectorising of seismograms based on Python programming. The objective was to develop and test this algorithm, which enables to digitise a massive archive dataset of seismograms automatically. 

\subsection{Goal}
The goal of our novel algorithm is to recognise specific features on the seismograms, i.e., to distinguish time intervals (minute/hour gaps/marks), annotations, time marks, and noise. Most of the existing R2V applications ignore specific features of seismograms: signal trace amplitude, strength and repeatability of vibration, time intervals (minute/hour gaps/marks recorded by the seismometer drum), handwritten technical annotations indicating time, and noise often in the edges of the paper (stains, spots, blurs).

Besides, the overlapped trace lines caused by the increased signals from the ground motion should be specially treated. The existing algorithms do not consider these variables, which makes vectorisation a mechanical process of line tracking. Instead, they are focused on simple graphics and drawing \cite{Filipski19921195,Kasturi} or scanned maps \cite{1285753,Spinello}. However, vectorising seismograms presents a more complex task, compared to line detection in drawing and graphics \cite{Greenlee,Hooi,BLACK198133}, due to the additional semantic components embedded in geophysical records. Thus, our goal is to overcome the existing gaps in vectorisation by considering features of seismograms.

\section{Methodology}
During the \nth{1} thesis year (2021/2022) we developed the approach of Python to a vectorisation of seismograms, which included trace line thinning, thresholding and detecting the time gaps to automatically recognise seismic traces on the images. A dataset of bitmap images containing scanned seismograms was processed by me using the code written on Python language by Prof. Dr. O. Debeir. The methods included automatic vectorising and converting the seismogram images from raster to vector format (R2V), detecting traces and identifying time gap intervals automatically. Thus, we proposed a novel Python-based algorithm to automatic vectorisation of the scanned analog seismograms.

\subsection{Workflow}
The general workflow of the work that we did during the \nth{1} thesis year (2021/2022) consists in large massif dataset processing. The workflow for this processing is outlined as followed: 
 
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \emph{Data organising --} The images were collected and organised in the Cytomine workspace. The dataset included 145 files with each ca. 300-400 MB, organised for viewing and analysis. The workload was divided and separated between the members of the SeismoStorm project. Thus, I uploaded raw data, Prof. Dr. O. Debeir performed metadata processing (ontology, generating time marks, general data management, etc). 	
	\subitem \emph{Cytomine for data management -- } The Cytomine workspace and editing system was used as a storage place and management system for a large dataset.
	\subitem \emph{Ontology classes in SeismoStorm -- } The ontology classes were generated using labeled features of objects on the images: seismic traces, time gaps with subclasses, trace segments, annotations, noise and defects, initial hour marks.
	\subitem \emph{Annotating the objects -- } The features on the images and associate terms were identified, annotated and described. These included time gaps, handwritten remarks and seismic traces.	
	\item \emph{Image capture -- } Raster images were grabbed by Python script using a path to the files located in the Cytomine. The images were fetched by the developed API enabling the access to the Cytomine, and stored in a working folder.	
	\item \emph{Detection of the ROI -- } The threshold of ROI was detected at low resolution by identifying a region of the scan occupied by signal. This improved the time of computing and optimised the use of memory during image processing.  	
	\item \emph{Tile processing -- } The ROI is then sliced into regular overlapping tiles. Tile size chosen is such that it fits easily into memory and is large enough to cover at least one and a half minute segment length, and the overlap is chosen to ensure that a minute segment is completely included in one tile. 	
	\subitem \emph{Segmentation -- } The segmentation of the line is applied to each tile extracted from the ROI. The lines on the seismograms were thinned using the cycles of algorithm executed iteratively until only the skeleton of line remained. The following parameters were tested: number of pixels forming the line, radius of pixel's neighbourhood and threshold of the intensity of grey (level of colour in pixels considered or ignored).	
	\subitem \emph{Edge detection -- } The edges of seismic traces (start and end points) were detected according to the seismogram structure: minute and hour time gaps made by the drum of the seismometer which signify the ends of the line segments. 
	\subitem \emph{Vectorisation -- } Each tile on the image was digitised by an algorithm configured as a loop of code processing the image. This resulted in digitised segments visualised as vector lines on the seismograms, coloured randomly.  	
	\item \emph{Line approximation --} The lines were interpolated as several short segments of splines, separated by the minute gaps between each segment. 	
	\item \emph{Creating buffer zones for line tracking -- } The minute segments were traced and marked as parts constituting the hour on each seismogram. This resulted in a matrix of pixels stored in a memory of the program.	
	\item \emph{Time labelling --} Time gaps were detected as hour and minute intervals regularly breaking the line.  Timing of hour marks was done using detected double gaps located closely to the first minute of the consecutive hour. Annotating the time intervals was done using minute and hour gaps by detecting repeated breaks of seismic traces.	
	\item \emph{Corrections --} Validation of errors was performed by the double line vectorisation using the overlapping time periods. This was only necessary for the border regions where neighbouring tiles cross each other with a one-minute interval.	
	\item \emph{Conversion and plotting --} The sequence of pixels forming the segments of vector lines was converted  into the vector format. The graphs of the vectorised lines were visualised using the Matplotlib library.
\end{enumerate}

I used this workflow for processing large dataset containing seismograms with aim to convert them from raster to vector format using novel Python-based algorithm.

\subsection{Algorithm}

\subsubsection{Image capture}

The images were captured using API script from the original location in a SeismoStorm workspace of Cytomine and read into Python platform. The tiles were downloaded using the defined credentials, image IDs and the access to path in the Cytomine. Since the resolution of each image is high, the size of the images (ca. 400 MB each) did not technically allow to process them simultaneously due to the high amount of the required computer memory. Therefore, each image was divided into the several tiles with defined dimensions which were digitised separately. The tiles had the following dimensions: width=4 minutes, height=1024 pixels, the overlap between the two images 1.1\% * 1.1\% minute. The horizontal gap of the overlap between the images (interline) is 200 pixels. One minute on a seismogram corresponds to the 1400 pixels in the original TIFF. 

\subsection{Detection of ROI}
To optimise the data processing and to avoid empty spaces on the edges of the paper, the original images were cropped by defining the threshold of pixels in the Region of Interest (ROI). Thresholding crops the image using the values of pixels' colour and intensity \cite{GonzalezWoods}. The process is based on separating of the pixel's colours: dark objects (traces) were separated from the white background. It classifies pixels into those above the threshold as 'background' (empty edges) and those lesser than a threshold as 'target class objects' (seismogram traces). The procedure was performed using RANSAC (RANdom SAmple Consensus) algorithm \cite{FischlerBolles}, commonly used in image processing, which is an iterative method to estimate the parameters of the robust model which includes input data with outliers (noise). The aim of RANSAC is to select the correct inlier correspondences given a set of the one-to-one matches. In our model, the fit() function of RANSAC was used to fit the tiles to given target values of the tile period $ransac.fit(x\_hours, y\_hours)$.

The boolean array of inliers was defined by mask: $inlier\_mask = ransac.inlier\_mask\_$ and $outlier\_mask = np.logical\_not(inlier\_mask)$. The search for the closest segment in the sequence of tiles on a seismogram was always starting to the right (new next minute), then the next minutes. Accordingly, the search for the closest segment was starting to the left (minute 0 of the hour) then the previous minutes. In such a way, the algorithm propagated along a line of the seismogram, searching for every consecutive minute iteratively. Minute marks and hour gaps were evenly spaced within an interval of time covering each seismogram using NumPy as follows: $line_X = np.arange(x\_hours.min(), x\_hours.max())[:, np.newaxis]$. For vertically-oriented images the rotation was done using numpy.transpose module of NumPy which enables to reverse the axes of an array of pixels.

The detection of the ROI was performed using thresholding which separated the main part on the image with meaningful data from the ignored part that contains noise. Thresholding partitioned the image using pixels' values of colour intensity. The iterative algorithms separated the image into the two parts and divided all the pixels into the two groups: with colour intensity above and below the threshold. Fig. \ref{fig:04} illustrates the process. It shows the seismogram (left) and its enlarged fragment of the selected region with vectorised traces (right). The bottom row of the figure presents histograms used for detecting ROI by thresholding. 

The pixels on the seismogram with values excessed threshold level indicated noise and were excluded from the data analysis (those above the red dotted line). The area between the red dashed lines shows the ROI, Fig. \ref{fig:04}. In visualisation, the pixels located within the ROI were used for data processing, while those beyond the ROI were excluded since their values exceeded the threshold line, as shown in histograms as red line in the bottom row of Fig. \ref{fig:04}). We repetitively changed the combinations of pixels' radius and line thickness to find the optimal combination. The pairing was performed without replacements and visualised as a continuous plot (Fig. \ref{fig:05}) in order to visually compare the outputs. The sample results show the images representing trials of the line thickness for various combinations of radius, number of pixels in a line, and percentage (\%) of the intensity of grey in pixel’s colour.

Similar to the studies on object recognition \cite{Carmichael,Pernici}, the classifiers are used to define the threshold of objects' parameters. Afterwards, the algorithm performed the comparison of the actual parameters within the threshold and did the partition of the image by assigning/rejecting pixels to/from the class of 'lines'. The object tracking was done iteratively until the whole seismogram is classified and lines were detected. Using the defined parameters the pixels were distinguished from the background using objects' appearance learned previously from the definitions of the threshold. The final data structure is presented by the vector data that can be overlaid on the original scanned TIFFs for quality control and visual inspections. The overlay of data was performed in the Cytomine environment. 

\subsection{Tile processing} 
Each image contained 180 tiles which were processed individually. Selecting the tiles from the original image was technically done using linear\_model of Scikit-Learn library (sklearn.linear\_model.RANSACRegressor). The  KDTree class of scipy.spatial module was used for processing structures of spatial data. The processing workflow for each tile included segmentation of line on each tile extracted from the ROI, edge detection, vectorisation of individual traces and indicating times, by creating buffers for minute intervals and detecting time gaps for one tile on a seismogram. The segmentation of the line is applied to each tile extracted from the ROI. The lines on the seismograms were thinned using the cycles of algorithm executed iteratively until only the skeleton of line remained. The following parameters were tested: number of pixels forming the line, radius of pixel's neighbourhood and threshold of colour intensity in pixels.

The edges of seismic traces (start and end points) were detected according to the structure of seismogram: minute and hour time gaps made by the drum of the seismometer which signify the ends of the line segments. Each tile on the image was digitised individually. This resulted in digitised segments visualised as vectors on seismograms. The interpretation of the straight or slightly curved lines performed well, while overlapping of lines required attention when defining the parameters for automatic line detection. The overlap cases existed between the two neighbour tiles, defined as 1.1\% of minute. In these cases, border traces were digitised twice. 

The correction of such cases was done semi-automatically by checking the border regions and ignoring the overlapped vector segments in neighbouring tiles. Each image contained 180 tiles processed individually during 10 seconds for each tile. The total time processing for one seismogram is ca. 30 minutes, which demonstrates a high time efficiency of the proposed algorithm. The algorithm was tested initially on a single image processed as a complete workflow and then applied for the dataset. 

\subsection{Line approximation and vectorisation}
The lines were interpolated as several short segments of splines, separated by the minute gaps between each segment and vecrorised. The interpretation of the image on the pixel-based level is made by the machine algorithm of Python \cite{van1995python}, which assesses the curvature and colour of he pixels. The decision in pixel grouping is made using features of these pixels that match the target classes ('line'/'not line'): colour, distance to the threshold in pixels, angle of line direction, etc). The shape of the line as a vector geometric structure is recognised and identified by the machine vision, based on the assessment of the connectivity of pixels constituting the line, expect for the time gaps and marks breaking the trace in case of seismograms. 

The aim of the vectorisation algorithm is to transform the original raster pixels representing the line into a vector object with the centralised skeleton. The tested seismogram has been extracted from the original raster image with a coarse resolution. The vectorisation was done by tiles and resulted in the sequence of vector traces (minute segments). Complex cases in vectorising process included overlap of lines, double vectorisation of the same line located on the border between the two tiles because of the different paths to this line.

\subsection{Creating buffer zones for line tracking}

Detecting timing gap intervals through buffering was done around hour and minute marks. The features of the objects were defined used to train the classifiers to discriminate segments of seismic lines on the images. This process was based on the colours properties of pixels which were used to identify buffers for time gaps (break of dark line and light colour of the background).

\subsection{Time labelling}
The time gaps were identified dividing the line into repetitive segments with regular intervals, Fig. \ref{fig:08}. Timing of hour marks was done using the detected double gaps located closely to the first minute of the following hour. The KDTree class from scipy.spatial Python module was used for automatic generation of time stamps based on the nearest neighbors search algorithm. Annotating the time intervals was performed using minute and hour gaps by identifying the breaks of seismic traces. The buffer zones of 500 pixels were created around the target pixel of each hour/minute gap to detect time marks, Fig. \ref{fig:08}. This buffer represents the zone of influence for each pixel in order to accurately find minute marks. The algorithm performed vectorising and timing of each tile in 10 seconds and processed seismograms in both vertical and horizontal orientation, Fig. \ref{fig:08}.

Hour gaps were detected based on frequency as small breaks on the lines encountered once on each line segment: the location of each hour mark was identified based on its location within the seismogram. Along the path of the line, each pixel represents a central point of the line in a given transect directed perpendicular to the line path. By moving along its path in a continuous way, these pixels are connected. A series of these pixels forms a shape of the vector line in a continuous way. Thus, the contour of the line is presented by the centroids of the original lines on raster images plotted on the scanned image by the recorder of the seismometer. This enabled to perform tracking of seismic lines based on their discriminating from the noise and background using parameters of colour of pixels.

\subsection{Empirical and/or theoretical approaches}
In contrast to the existing traditional solutions (we compared the novel code with the performance of DigitSeis software), our algorithm presented several advantages. First, it enable to vectorise lines in both vertical and horizontal oriented modes. Besides, the algorithm empirically identifies trace segments and time intervals on the images using buffers in an automated mode. Finally, the vectorising workflow has a higher accuracy and speed of data processing compared to the state-of-the-art (DigitSeis) software. 

High vectorising precision is achieved due to the theoretical approach of algorithm, which based on the recognition of pixels' colour. This enabled to accurately discriminate traces, ignore stained background and maintain geometric shape and topology of seismic lines. Novel algorithm performed vectorising of each tile in 10 seconds and processing of seismograms oriented in both vertical and horizontal directions, which proved a high speed and effectiveness.

Title: « Extracting microseismic ground motion from legacy seismograms ».
Authors: Olivier Debeir 1, Polina Lemenkova1, Raphael S. M. De Plaen 2, and Thomas Lecocq 2 
Affiliations:1 Université Libre de Bruxelles, École polytechnique de Bruxelles, Laboratory of Image Synthesis and Analysis. Campus de Solbosch, Av. Franklin D. Abstract: Before digital recordings became available in the 1970s, the ground motion was recorded using ink on white paper, scratching black-smoked paper, or light on photographic paper. While those analog seismic records offer unique continuous observations from the last century, most of them are now stacked and archived in boxes and potentially exposed to physical decay and permanent loss. To preserve those records and ultimately subject them to modern methods of analysis, it is time-sensitive to scan and digitize them. Here, we worked on a method for automatic digitization of paper seismograms using image processing and machine learning to extract microseismic ground-motion periods and amplitudes. We implemented the method on legacy data recorded at the Royal Observatory of Belgium to extract power spectral densities for major storms during the last century, which are compared with modeled microseisms levels computed using a numerical ocean wave model. This further shows how digitizing analog seismograms does not only preserve the scientific legacy but also makes new research possible by bringing analog data to the digital age.

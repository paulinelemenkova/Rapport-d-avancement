\section{Thesis topic}
\subsection{Thesis title}
Exploitation of historical analog seismological records by image processing and machine learning

\subsection{Research focus}
My PhD thesis is focused on processing of paper-based scanned seismograms using machine learning (ML) tools. Seismograms are the key data source in computational geophysics and seismology. Historical seismograms recorded in \nth{20} century form valuable data pool to evaluate past ground motions and seismicity of the Earth. However, such data mostly remain as old papers or in a raster format (scanned images), which should be vectorised to enable reuse. At the same time, manual vectorisation is a highly time‐consuming and cumbersome work, prone to human-induced errors, which often leads to misclassification of seismic traces. To address these limitations, we developed the Python-based algorithm of automatic vectorisation of the seismograms. 

\subsection{Research questions}
The research questions that we address in my PhD thesis include the following ones:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item What are the efficient ML methods for automatic vectorising of seismograms? 
    \item Which libraries and embedded algorithms of Python language can be applied for automatic vectorisation ?
    \item How to assess and interpret signals using seismic data in a semi-automated regime? 
    \item How to effectively process and organise massive datasets containing large amounts of seismograms ?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research problem} 

The research problem consists in developing effective method for vectorisation of seismograms. Seismograms are the key source of information in many geophysical applications and Earth science domains, such as earthquake modelling \cite{Kameda}, risk assessment \cite{Frankel,Panakkat}, civil engineering \cite{Savvaidis}, soil studies \cite{Papadakis}, geological simulations on rock and mineral physics, porosity and rheology \cite{Singhetal2020,DiMartino}. The analysis of the seismograms can be used for reconstructions of the past seismicity of the Earth, retrospective data modelling and earthquake hazard prediction \cite{Zhuetal2021}.  

So far, there is no effective method for robust vectorisation of seismograps. There are existing software for digitising seismograms, their solutions. For instance, Bogiatzis and Ishii developed the \emph{DigitSeis} \cite{Bogiatzis} aimed to classify features on seismograms, discriminating noise and generating time series to assign timing. Pintore et al. \cite{Pintoreetal2005} developed the \emph{Teseo} package where curves are recognised using colour contrast, approximated and vectorised. However, these software have technical limitations and drawbacks. For example, the success in vectorising seismograms by these software requires high quality images as input data. Ideally, they should have a distinct colour on the paper, contrasting and clearly recognised lines, no defects and faults, blurs and spots, distinguishable line width and continuity, identical time gaps or marks and line styles (curvature, repetitive chains of segments). In case of a perfect image, using such tools may lead to the acceptable outputs. Nevertheless, in reality old scanned images are almost often imperfect and need using machine learning algorithm for an automatic vectorisation that could ignore the imperfectness of the old image. 

In contrast, a ML-based algorithm can vectorise blurred or imperfect images, that is, those that contain spots and stains, where manual vectorising fails. Besides the questions of paper quality, processing the seismograms involves analysis of their amplitude, record time and signals source, originating from horizontal or vertical components. Therefore, it becomes a cumbersome task to vectorise images using traditional software. Automated workflow outperforms such methods by recognising features contained in a specific format of seismograms using computer vision and ML algorithms. This is possible using raster-to-vector (R2V) digitising methods.

Many R2V methods were proposed for optimisation of the vectorisation algorithms. Some of them employ ML for improving the accuracy and accelerating the speed of vectorising \cite{SilvaCoira,Mineter,Tafoya,Doucette}. Along with the progress in programming, such methods gradually improved and resulted in a variety of the developed algorithms of vectorisation, e.g., threshold morphology \cite{Janssen1997}, real-time line extraction \cite{Pferd,FULFORD198165}, topological analysis \cite{GROSS1995370}, approximation \cite{StanberryBesag,WallDanielsson}, 3D reconstruction \cite{Ceylan}. More examples of R2V algorithms include recognising the lines and discriminating them from the white/light background by line thinning, finding edges and neighbouring nodes \cite{Suzuki}, contrasting line width and smoothing shapes \cite{BaoFu}, iterative line optimisation and segmentation by CNN \cite{Inoue}, layering image and segmenting skeletons \cite{Hilaire}, extracting contours by simplification \cite{San,Chenetal2006}. 

\subsection{Motivation}
 The motivation to vectorise old scanned seismograms collected from the historical archives comes from the value of the seismic records for geophysical research. Seismograms reflect the characteristics of body and surface seismic waves of the Earth \cite{Kawai,Kennett}. They may originate from various types of sources corresponding to the geodynamic processes. Most often recording earthquake strength \cite{Krishna2006}, they can also indicate other types of disasters, e.g., avalanches, tsunami, volcanic eruptions \cite{Vijaya2015}. Furthermore, signals strength and amplitudes of seismic waves recorded in seismograms are used to determine the ground motion of the Earth using specific properties and nature of seismic waves which includes the geometry of movements \cite{Lamb,Liuetal2016,Mueller,Fryer}. Records of the horizontal components in seismograms show regional heterogeneity and variations in the elastic parameters of the Earth's mantle \cite{Melchior}. Moreover, seismic wave velocity is strongly related to the tectonic processes \cite{Leveque,Butler} and characteristics of the lithosphere \cite{Knopoff,Singh}, reflect regional geologic structure \cite{gupta19663,Mitchell} and geodynamic characteristics of shallow soil layer \cite{Verachtert}. 

\subsection{Methodology}
During the \nth{1} thesis year (2021/2022) we developed the approach of Python to a vectorisation of seismograms, which included trace line thinning, thresholding and detecting the time gaps to automatically recognise seismic traces on the images. A dataset of bitmap images containing scanned seismograms was processed by me using the code written on Python language by Prof. Dr. O. Debeir. The methods included automatic vectorising and converting the seismogram images from raster to vector format (R2V), detecting traces and identifying time gap intervals automatically. Thus, we proposed a novel Python-based algorithm to automatic vectorisation of the scanned analog seismograms.

\subsection{Objective}
During the \nth{1} thesis year (2021/2022) we addressed a problem of vectorising seismograms by a novel algorithm for automated vectorising of seismograms based on Python programming. The objective was to develop and test this algorithm, which enables to digitise a massive archive dataset of seismograms automatically. 

\subsection{Goal}
The goal of our novel algorithm is to recognise specific features on the seismograms, i.e., to distinguish time intervals (minute/hour gaps/marks), annotations, time marks, and noise. Most of the existing R2V applications ignore specific features of seismograms: signal trace amplitude, strength and repeatability of vibration, time intervals (minute/hour gaps/marks recorded by the seismometer drum), handwritten technical annotations indicating time, and noise often in the edges of the paper (stains, spots, blurs). Besides, the overlapped trace lines caused by the increased signals from the ground motion should be specially treated. The existing algorithms do not consider these variables, which makes vectorisation a mechanical process of line tracking. Instead, they are focused on simple graphics and drawing \cite{Filipski19921195,Kasturi} or scanned maps \cite{1285753,Spinello}. However, vectorising seismograms presents a more complex task, compared to line detection in drawing and graphics \cite{Greenlee,Hooi,BLACK198133}, due to the additional semantic components embedded in geophysical records. Thus, our goal is to overcome the existing gaps in vectorisation by considering features of seismograms.

\section{Summary of the obtained results}
I tested the code and performed data analysis using the novel Python-based algorithm written by O. Debeir. This algorithm enabled to automatically convert the bitmap graphics into a vector format using the R2V method which I processed at LISA. The Python was used because of its high computational functionality, logic and flexibility of syntax \cite{van1995python} and demonstrated robust results, according to the validation and quality control. 

\subsection{Workflow}
The general workflow of the work that we did during the \nth{1} thesis year (2021/2022) consists in large massif dataset processing. The workflow for this processing is outlined as followed: 
 
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \emph{Data organising --} The images were collected and organised in the Cytomine workspace. The dataset included 145 files with each ca. 300-400 MB, organised for viewing and analysis. The workload was divided and separated between the members of the SeismoStorm project. Thus, I uploaded raw data, Prof. Dr. O. Debeir performed metadata processing (ontology, generating time marks, general data management, etc). 	
	\subitem \emph{Cytomine for data management -- } The Cytomine workspace and editing system was used as a storage place and management system for a large dataset.
	\subitem \emph{Ontology classes in SeismoStorm -- } The ontology classes were generated using labeled features of objects on the images: seismic traces, time gaps with subclasses, trace segments, annotations, noise and defects, initial hour marks.
	\subitem \emph{Annotating the objects -- } The features on the images and associate terms were identified, annotated and described. These included time gaps, handwritten remarks and seismic traces.	
	\item \emph{Image capture -- } Raster images were grabbed by Python script using a path to the files located in the Cytomine. The images were fetched by the developed API enabling the access to the Cytomine, and stored in a working folder.	
	\item \emph{Detection of the ROI -- } The threshold of ROI was detected at low resolution by identifying a region of the scan occupied by signal. This improved the time of computing and optimised the use of memory during image processing.  	
	\item \emph{Tile processing -- } The ROI is then sliced into regular overlapping tiles. Tile size chosen is such that it fits easily into memory and is large enough to cover at least one and a half minute segment length, and the overlap is chosen to ensure that a minute segment is completely included in one tile. 	
	\subitem \emph{Segmentation -- } The segmentation of the line is applied to each tile extracted from the ROI. The lines on the seismograms were thinned using the cycles of algorithm executed iteratively until only the skeleton of line remained. The following parameters were tested: number of pixels forming the line, radius of pixel's neighbourhood and threshold of the intensity of grey (level of colour in pixels considered or ignored).	
	\subitem \emph{Edge detection -- } The edges of seismic traces (start and end points) were detected according to the seismogram structure: minute and hour time gaps made by the drum of the seismometer which signify the ends of the line segments. 
	\subitem \emph{Vectorisation -- } Each tile on the image was digitised by an algorithm configured as a loop of code processing the image. This resulted in digitised segments visualised as vector lines on the seismograms, coloured randomly.  	
	\item \emph{Line approximation --} The lines were interpolated as several short segments of splines, separated by the minute gaps between each segment. 	
	\item \emph{Creating buffer zones for line tracking -- } The minute segments were traced and marked as parts constituting the hour on each seismogram. This resulted in a matrix of pixels stored in a memory of the program.	
	\item \emph{Time labelling --} Time gaps were detected as hour and minute intervals regularly breaking the line.  Timing of hour marks was done using detected double gaps located closely to the first minute of the consecutive hour. Annotating the time intervals was done using minute and hour gaps by detecting repeated breaks of seismic traces.	
	\item \emph{Corrections --} Validation of errors was performed by the double line vectorisation using the overlapping time periods. This was only necessary for the border regions where neighbouring tiles cross each other with a one-minute interval.	
	\item \emph{Conversion and plotting --} The sequence of pixels forming the segments of vector lines was converted  into the vector format. The graphs of the vectorised lines were visualised using the Matplotlib library.
\end{enumerate}

I used this workflow for processing large dataset containing seismograms with aim to convert them from raster to vector format using novel Python-based algorithm. 

\subsection{Data processing}

\subsubsection{Data scanning and formatting}
Data processing was performed during winter 2021 (October/December 2021). We processed data scanning (performed at ROB) and stored them in digital bitmap format. The images were scanned with a high resolution (600 dpi) and A0 format using a large-format scanning device HD Ultra X Series (36'', 42'')/HD Ultra X ScanStation. The seismograms were recorded by the Galitzine seismometer, an electromagnetic instrument developed in 1910 to record a single component (horizontal or vertical) of ground motions. The data were recorded by the drum of the seismometer and stored in boxes in a storage space of the ROB and reflect the seismicity of the Earth. 

The images were then converted into bitmap 0/1 (white/black) and saved as files in graphical bitmap formats: TIFF, JPG and PNG. Most of the data were stored in TIFF format which present a matrix of 2D arrays of pixels assigned to a value in a gray scale. The files were stored with metadata summarised for our paper which is in progress of \emph{Scientific Reports} journal (planned). The images were stored with a resolution of 300 dpi, ca. 350-400 MB size each, in 256 B/W monochrome scales. We also performed visual data analysis and inspection: the records from the Galitzine seismometer are monochrome, while those of Wiecherts are in colour (ivory background). The dataset included 145 images of the analogue seismograms in a raster format. 
 
 \subsubsection{Data management and organisation}
During December 2021 and January 2022 we performed data processing in the Cytomine workspace and editing system for interactive data storage and management. The files were uploaded in Cytomine (\href{https://cytomine.com/}{Cytomine}). where we kept and processed images using the following functionality: organising and navigating the data, adding metadata and annotations, visualising and analysing the images, basic image manipulations (rapid view, zooming and rotating). The technical details of the images were recored in metadata and included information regarding the resolution, size, perimeter and area in pixels. As a workspace, Cytomine provides storage place for a large dataset. We uploaded and organised 145 files, each ca. 300-400 MB of size.

To test this code, we used archived dataset obtained from the Royal Observatory of Belgium, Uccle seismic station, scanned as high‐resolution images (over 100 high-resolution files). Thus, the advantages of the developed algorithm include high speed and automation of data processing, accuracy and robustness of the vectorisation results.

\subsection{Achieved results}
During the academic year 2021/2022 I processed the dataset containing seismic records from Uccle seismic station of the Royal Observatory of Belgium (ROB). The dataset included over 100 scanned images of archived seismograms recorded in 1954. The data coverage period is from 1 January 1954 to 12 March 1954. For data vectorisation, processing and plotting I used the algorithm written on Python by Prof. Dr. O. Debeir. Vectorised seismograms reflect the ground motions, so we plan to proceed with geophysical evaluation of the data values.

\subsection{Empirical and/or theoretical approaches}
In contrast to the existing traditional solutions (we compared the novel code with the performance of DigitSeis software), our algorithm presented several advantages. First, it enable to vectorise lines in both vertical and horizontal oriented modes. Besides, the algorithm empirically identifies trace segments and time intervals on the images using buffers in an automated mode. Finally, the vectorising workflow has a higher accuracy and speed of data processing compared to the state-of-the-art (DigitSeis) software. 

High vectorising precision is achieved due to the theoretical approach of algorithm, which based on the recognition of pixels' colour. This enabled to accurately discriminate traces, ignore stained background and maintain geometric shape and topology of seismic lines. Novel algorithm performed vectorising of each tile in 10 seconds and processing of seismograms oriented in both vertical and horizontal directions, which proved a high speed and effectiveness.

\section{Publications}
Conference paper accepted at 3ECEES (The Third European Conference on Earthquake Engineering and Seismology), \href{https://3ecees.ro/}{https://3ecees.ro/}.
Title: « Extracting microseismic ground motion from legacy seismograms ».
Authors: Olivier Debeir 1, Polina Lemenkova1, Raphael S. M. De Plaen 2, and Thomas Lecocq 2 
Affiliations:1 Université Libre de Bruxelles, École polytechnique de Bruxelles, Laboratory of Image Synthesis and Analysis. Campus de Solbosch, Av. Franklin D. Roosevelt 50, Brussels, BE-1000, Belgium. 2 Royal Observatory of Belgium, Seismology \& Gravimetry (OD2). Av. Circulaire 3, Uccle, BE-1180, Belgium
Location: Palace of the Parliament -- Bucharest International Conference Centre Bucharest, Bucharest, Romania.   
Organizers: The National Institute for Earth Physics (INFP) and Technical University of Civil Engineering Bucharest (UTCB).
Date: September 4-9, 2022.                                
Abstract: Before digital recordings became available in the 1970s, the ground motion was recorded using ink on white paper, scratching black-smoked paper, or light on photographic paper. While those analog seismic records offer unique continuous observations from the last century, most of them are now stacked and archived in boxes and potentially exposed to physical decay and permanent loss. To preserve those records and ultimately subject them to modern methods of analysis, it is time-sensitive to scan and digitize them. Here, we worked on a method for automatic digitization of paper seismograms using image processing and machine learning to extract microseismic ground-motion periods and amplitudes. We implemented the method on legacy data recorded at the Royal Observatory of Belgium to extract power spectral densities for major storms during the last century, which are compared with modeled microseisms levels computed using a numerical ocean wave model. This further shows how digitizing analog seismograms does not only preserve the scientific legacy but also makes new research possible by bringing analog data to the digital age. Topics: earthquake engineering engineering seismology earthquake disaster mitigation and management seismology and seismotectonics.
